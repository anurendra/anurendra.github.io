Bio

Postdoctoral Researcher, Stanford University. Mechanism-aware AI for genomics and cardiovascular drug discovery.

I am driven by a simple question: how can we use complex genomic data to understand why disease unfolds the way it does, and how can that understanding lead to better treatment? Two kinds of evidence bear on this, and each is half an answer. Patients tell us which variants and which molecular programs track with disease, but little about why. Cells tell us mechanism, but not whether that mechanism matters at the level of an organism. I build methods that join the two, and keep them close enough to experiment to be proven wrong. Over twenty years, in cardiovascular disease, I intend for a drug to come out the other end.

Joining them is hard because models in genomics break when you move them: a new cell type, a new patient, a new perturbation, and performance quietly collapses. My working position is that mechanism is what buys generalization, and that without it we will keep hitting this wall. In modeling terms it is an inductive bias, and one of the few with a plausible claim to being true. I build that structure in, then try hard to break it.

In practice this means modeling disease programs from single-cell and spatial transcriptomics and linking them to interventions, separating disease-specific signal from shared remodeling so that a target list reflects what actually drives the phenotype. My focus is genetic cardiac diseases, particularly dilated cardiomyopathy. The harder question is transport. Nearly all large-scale perturbation data lives in cancer cell lines, because that is what is cheap to screen; the cells that matter are cardiomyocytes. What survives that gap is the mechanistic part. I work closely with experimentalists so that predictions meet iPSC-derived models quickly enough to fail fast.

A parallel thread runs throughout: characterizing where these methods fail. Much of the day-to-day work is statistical: building null models that respect how tissue is actually organized, checking whether an effect survives conditioning on composition, asking whether a benchmark's ground truth is circular. Spatial data is dominated by structured confounding. Tissue architecture and local composition manufacture convincing patterns, and conditioning on them changes the biology you conclude. That finding drove my PhD work on subcellular RNA organization (InSTAnT, Nature Communications 2024) and on cell–cell communication in spatial data (CellWHISPER, bioRxiv 2026). The same instinct runs through my current work on variant effect prediction, where the failure modes of widely used predictors turn out to be correlated rather than independent, so ensembling does not rescue them.

I have also interned at Genentech, Vertex, IBM Research (4×), and others, and briefly explored a startup, experiences that taught me a great deal about building end-to-end systems.
