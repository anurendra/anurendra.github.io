Postdoctoral Scholar, Stanford University. Mechanism-aware AI for genomics.

Models in genomics break when you move them. I work on why.

I am driven by a simple question: how can we use complex genomic data to understand why disease unfolds the way it does, and how can that understanding lead to better treatment? Two kinds of data speak to this, and they sit at opposite ends of the same causal chain.

A perturbation screen gives a cause and an effect two days apart. High resolution, no outcome.

Human genetics gives a cause and an effect four decades apart. A real intervention, a real endpoint, nothing in between.

The middle is missing. Mechanism is what fills it.

I work at the level of cells and genes, the finest resolution at which that chain is still visible, modeling how cells communicate and what they set off downstream. My working hypothesis is that mechanism is what buys generalization: in modeling terms an inductive bias, and one of the few with a plausible claim to being true. Statistics is the other half of the method, and tells me when the data cannot support the structure I want to impose. Over twenty years, in cardiovascular disease, I intend for a drug to come out the other end.

The recurring problem is that models break the moment you move them. A new cell type, a new patient, a new perturbation, and performance quietly collapses. My working hypothesis is that mechanism is what buys generalization, and that without it we will keep hitting this wall. In modeling terms it is an inductive bias, and one of the few with a plausible claim to being true. The rest of the discipline is statistical: knowing when the data cannot support the structure you would like to impose.

Most of this sits in genetic cardiomyopathy. Nearly all large-scale perturbation data lives in cancer cell lines, because that is what is cheap to screen; the cells that matter are cardiomyocytes. Working out what carries across that gap, and what does not, is where I am actively focusing on.

This is where most of my work has gone. CellWHISPER (bioRxiv 2026) recovers contact-mediated signaling from spatial transcriptomics, and the same approach now runs through my work on cardiac organoids. Behind it is a finding from my PhD: spatial data is dominated by structured confounding, where tissue architecture and local composition manufacture convincing patterns, and conditioning on them changes the biology you conclude (InSTAnT, Nature Communications 2024). Much of the day-to-day work is that kind of checking, whether an effect survives conditioning on composition, whether a benchmark's ground truth is circular. The same instinct runs through my current work on variant effect prediction, where the failure modes of widely used predictors turn out to be correlated rather than independent, so ensembling does not rescue them.

Previously at Genentech, Vertex, and IBM Research, with a brief detour into building a startup, where I learned what it takes to make an end-to-end system actually work.
