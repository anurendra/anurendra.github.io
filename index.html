Postdoctoral Scholar, Stanford University. Mechanism-aware AI for genomics.

Models in genomics break when you move them. I work on why.

I am driven by a simple question: how can we use complex genomic data to understand why disease unfolds the way it does, and how can that understanding lead to better treatment? Two kinds of data speak to this, and they sit at opposite ends of the same causal chain.

A perturbation screen gives a cause and an effect two days apart. High resolution, no outcome.

Human genetics gives a cause and an effect four decades apart. A real intervention, a real endpoint, nothing in between.

The middle is missing. Mechanism is what fills it.

I model at the level of cells and genes, the finest resolution at which that chain is still visible, and I work with experimentalists so that what comes out gets tested. Over twenty years, in cardiovascular disease, I intend for a drug to come out the other end.

The recurring problem is that models break the moment you move them. A new cell type, a new patient, a new perturbation, and performance quietly collapses. My working hypothesis is that mechanism is what buys generalization, and that without it we will keep hitting this wall. In modeling terms it is an inductive bias, and one of the few with a plausible claim to being true. The rest of the discipline is statistical: knowing when the data cannot support the structure you would like to impose.

My focus is genetic cardiac disease, particularly dilated cardiomyopathy: modeling disease programs from single-cell and spatial transcriptomics and linking them to interventions, separating disease-specific signal from shared remodeling so that a target list reflects what actually drives the phenotype. The harder question is transport. Nearly all large-scale perturbation data lives in cancer cell lines, because that is what is cheap to screen; the cells that matter are cardiomyocytes.

A parallel thread runs throughout: characterizing where these methods fail. Much of the day-to-day work is statistical, building null models that respect how tissue is actually organized, checking whether an effect survives conditioning on composition, asking whether a benchmark's ground truth is circular. Spatial data is dominated by structured confounding. Tissue architecture and local composition manufacture convincing patterns, and conditioning on them changes the biology you conclude. That finding drove my PhD work on subcellular RNA organization (InSTAnT, Nature Communications 2024) and on cell–cell communication in spatial data (CellWHISPER, bioRxiv 2026). The same instinct runs through my current work on variant effect prediction, where the failure modes of widely used predictors turn out to be correlated rather than independent, so ensembling does not rescue them.

Previously at Genentech, Vertex, and IBM Research, with a brief detour into building a startup, where I learned what it takes to make an end-to-end system actually work.
