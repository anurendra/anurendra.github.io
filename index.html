Bio

Postdoctoral Researcher, Stanford University: mechanism-aware AI for genomics and cardiovascular drug discovery.

I am driven by a simple question: how can we use complex genomic data to understand why disease unfolds the way it does, and how can that understanding lead to better treatment? We can now generate far more biological hypotheses than we can test, so the bottleneck has moved from having ideas to deciding which ones deserve an experiment. I want to build the computational layer that makes that call well, and keep it close enough to experiment that it can be proven wrong. Over twenty years, in cardiovascular diseases, I would like a drug to come out the other end.

That call is hard to make because models in genomics break when you move them: a new cell type, a new patient, a new perturbation, and performance quietly collapses. I think mechanism is what buys you generalization: a model that encodes something about how signalling actually propagates will transfer, and one that pattern-matches will not. So I build that structure in, and then try hard to break it.

In practice that means modeling disease programs from single-cell and spatial transcriptomics and linking them to interventions: separating disease-specific signal from shared remodeling, so the target list reflects what is actually driving the phenotype. Mostly in genetic cardiac disease, particularly dilated cardiomyopathy. The harder question is transport. Nearly all large-scale perturbation data lives in cancer cell lines, because that is what is cheap to screen; the cells we care about are cardiomyocytes. What survives that gap is, I would argue, the mechanistic part. I work closely with experimentalists so predictions meet iPSC-derived models quickly enough to fail fast.

A parallel thread runs through everything: characterizing where these methods fail. Spatial data is dominated by structured confounding — tissue architecture and local composition manufacture convincing patterns — and conditioning on it changes the biology you conclude. That was the finding behind my PhD work on subcellular RNA organization (InSTAnT, Nature Communications 2024) and on cell–cell communication in spatial data (CellWHISPER, bioRxiv 2026).

I have also interned at Genentech, Vertex, IBM Research (4×), and others, and briefly explored a startup, experiences that taught me a great deal about building end-to-end systems.
